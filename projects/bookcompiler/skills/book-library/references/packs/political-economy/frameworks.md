## Beautiful Deleveraging Balance

> Combines monetary, fiscal, and debt-restructuring actions so debt-to-income falls without an offsetting collapse in incomes.

- book: 
- lenses: economics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- A country clearly needs to deleverage but debt ratios are rising
- Nominal growth is far below nominal interest rates
- Bank balance sheets and bad debts are obstructing credit transmission

### Inputs

- Nominal growth and nominal interest rates
- Bad-debt stock and banking-sector solvency position
- Fiscal space and the current fiscal deficit
- Available policy levers and their institutional feasibility

### Steps

- Measure how far nominal growth sits below nominal interest rates.
- Assess whether the required reduction in debt-to-income is coming from income growth or from debt write-downs.
- Identify which of the classic policy levers are actionable, including bank nationalization and liquidity provision.
- Add structural reforms where labor-market or institutional rigidity is slowing recovery.
- Sequence the stimulation so that nominal growth converges toward nominal interest rates.
- Track whether debt-to-income falls at a sustainable rate rather than through income collapse.

### Outputs

- A lever-by-lever action plan
- A projected path for nominal growth versus nominal interest rates
- An attribution of debt-ratio change to growth, inflation, and debt change

### Failure modes

- Over-relying on austerity, which lowers income and can raise debt-to-GDP
- Under-sizing stimulus so that debt ratios keep compounding upward
- Pursuing reforms without the accompanying liquidity and solvency measures

### Counterexamples

- Austria in 2008 needed a deleveraging yet its debt-to-GDP rose 39% as incomes declined, a case where the balance was not achieved.

### Source citations

> Over the cycle, Norway was aggressive in managing its financial institutions and bad debts, pulling 4 out of 9 classic policy levers.
  -  · p.292
> even though Austria needed a deleveraging, its debt as a % GDP went up by 39% (32% annualized) as incomes declined.
  -  · p.311

## Credibility-Backed Currency Reform Test

> Evaluates whether a new or reformed currency can stabilize by testing issuing-institution credibility and the stock of currency promises.

- book: 
- lenses: economics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- A currency has collapsed and a replacement or hard-currency reform is proposed
- Monetization of debt is competing with deflation as the binding risk
- Confidence in the issuing institution is the binding constraint

### Inputs

- Convertibility terms of the new currency
- Stock of debt denominated in the new currency
- Central bank balance sheet and institutional standing

### Steps

- Specify exactly what the new currency is exchangeable for and at what ratio.
- Assess whether the currency rests on formal backing or on institutional credibility alone.
- Measure the stock of promises to deliver the currency, especially debt denominated in it.
- Determine whether the central bank would be forced to choose between inflationary monetization and deflation.
- Plan the withdrawal and phase-out of the old currency on a fixed schedule.
- Treat the currency reform as one piece of a broader reform process, not a stand-alone fix.

### Outputs

- A credibility assessment for the issuing institution
- A monetization-versus-deflation risk statement
- A sequencing plan for old-currency withdrawal

### Failure modes

- Expecting a new currency alone to create lasting stabilization
- Issuing the currency with a large stock of denominated promises and re-creating the original dilemma
- Overlooking the institutional credibility gap that formal backing cannot substitute for

### Counterexamples

- The German hyperinflation fix succeeded partly because very few promises to deliver the new currencies existed, a condition that may not be replicable where large legacy debts are outstanding.

### Source citations

> Currency depends on the credibility of the institutions issuing it.
  -  · p.104
> But as we will see, it took much more than a new currency to create a lasting stabilization.
  -  · p.104

## Crisis Political-Economy Risk Map

> Anticipates populism, protectionism, and regime change that typically follow severe downturns and uneven recoveries.

- book: 
- lenses: politics, economics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- A deep downturn has produced visible unemployment and hardship
- A recovery is perceived as uneven, with elites seen as prospering
- Policy makers are viewed as ineffectual

### Inputs

- Unemployment and hardship indicators
- Income and wealth inequality shares
- Public confidence in policy makers
- Existing tariff and immigration policy stance

### Steps

- Assess the severity and duration of the downturn and the depth of unemployment.
- Compare elite outcomes with broad household outcomes during any recovery.
- Measure inequality in both income and wealth shares and its trajectory.
- Gauge public confidence in policy makers and the availability of scapegoats.
- Expect tariff increases and immigration restrictions as the standard political response.
- Flag elevated regime-change risk where the three conditions coincide.

### Outputs

- A political risk rating with named channels
- A list of policy shifts to anticipate, including tariffs and immigration limits
- A historical comparison to interwar or similar episodes

### Failure modes

- Treating the interwar pattern as inevitable rather than typical
- Ignoring institutional checks that can absorb the pressure
- Conflating a protective tariff response with a durable trade realignment

### Counterexamples

- A severe downturn absorbed by a political environment with strong checks and balances and rapid, credible policy response need not produce regime change.

### Source citations

> Driven by weak economic conditions, an uneven recovery (in which the elite was perceived to be prospering while the common man was still struggling), and ineffectual policy makers, populism was a global phenomenon in the interwar period (the 1920s to the 1930s), leading to regime changes not only in the United States, but also in Germany, Italy, and Spain.
  -  · p.147
> As is common in severe economic downturns, protectionist and anti-immi-grant sentiment began to rise.
  -  · p.127

## Debt Cycle Phase Classifier

> Locates an economy on the archetypal big debt cycle by reading bubble gauges, easing gauges, and debt-service capacity together.

- book: 
- lenses: economics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- A boom has visibly accelerated and observers disagree about whether it is sustainable
- A bust is underway and the sequencing of policy responses is being decided
- Comparing a live case against historical cases in a compendium

### Inputs

- Debt and debt-service as a share of GDP
- Core inflation path and nominal short and long rates
- Equity prices, real FX, reserves, current account, and capital flows
- Credit-growth and money-supply measures

### Steps

- Assemble the standard indicator set used in the case chart decks for the country and period.
- Read the bubble and depression gauges relative to zero to judge the extent of the bubble and any crossing into or out of it.
- Read the tightening and easing gauge to establish where peak tightening and peak easing sit.
- Check whether growth is being financed by debt rather than productivity gains.
- Test whether debt-service burden can be met with current income and available credit.
- Assign the case to a cycle phase and note which historical cases match most closely.

### Outputs

- A phase label with supporting indicator readings
- A list of closest historical analogues
- A statement of which indicators are missing or unreliable

### Failure modes

- Treating gauge readings as precise rather than rough measures
- Anchoring on a single analogue whose data coverage differs
- Classifying too early when several series have no data available

### Counterexamples

- Cases where the compendium series are absent, as with Russia and Mexico chart decks marked 'No Data Available' for the yield curve, cannot support a phase label from gauges alone.

### Source citations

> For each gauge, the difference from zero conveys the extent of the bubble while the crossing above/below zero represents the shifting into or out of the bubble.
  -  · p.331
> During this stage, growth is increasingly financed by debt rather than productivity gains, and the country typically becomes highly reliant on foreign financing.
  -  · p.43

## External-Currency Fragility Screen

> Screens an emerging boom for the foreign-currency and external-financing vulnerabilities that turn downturns into crises.

- book: 
- lenses: economics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- An emerging economy is growing fast on capital inflows
- Local financial markets are shallow and domestic savings are limited
- Asset prices and spending are rising alongside currency appreciation

### Inputs

- Composition and currency denomination of external debt
- Reserves, current account, and capital inflows and outflows as shares of GDP
- Real effective exchange rate path
- Depth of the local financial system and scale of domestic savings

### Steps

- Check whether local borrowing is unavailable or distrusted, forcing foreign-currency issuance.
- Measure foreign-currency denominated debt relative to reserves and income.
- Assess how much the rising currency has already eroded the country's competitiveness.
- Track whether growth is increasingly debt-financed rather than productivity-driven.
- Estimate the currency move required to service obligations if the currency weakens.
- Flag the case as externally fragile where obligations and outflows coincide.

### Outputs

- A fragility rating with the specific binding exposure
- Stress estimates for debt service under currency depreciation
- A watchlist of reserve, current-account, and flow thresholds

### Failure modes

- Reading strong growth and rising asset prices as strength rather than as a borrowing-fueled bubble
- Ignoring the local-currency value of foreign-currency obligations
- Assuming foreign financing will remain available once sentiment turns

### Counterexamples

- Developed economies that borrow primarily in their own currency with deep domestic savings do not fit this screen and require a different vulnerability model.

## Restructuring Leverage Assessment

> Judges who holds real power in a debt restructuring by weighing the ability to inflict, reward, and endure pain.

- book: 
- lenses: politics, economics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- Multiple parties must agree on who bears losses
- The relative strength of creditors and debtors is contested
- Unsettled precedents leave the enforcement of terms unclear

### Inputs

- Each party's reserves, financing access, and alternative options
- Each party's tolerance for economic and social pain
- The range of untested enforcement mechanisms available

### Steps

- List what each party could force on the others if talks failed.
- Score each party's ability to endure pain, not only to inflict it.
- Identify untested ways of reward and punishment that make power ambiguous.
- Prefer a negotiated exchange when power is genuinely unclear.
- Where power is clear, expect the dominant party to set rules and the other to comply until the next cycle.
- In economic terms, prioritize maintaining access to financial resources through the negotiation.

### Outputs

- A relative-power ranking for each party
- A recommendation on negotiated versus imposed outcomes
- A list of the pain each party can absorb

### Failure modes

- Equating creditor size with actual coercive power
- Pushing too hard when power is untested and triggering a worse outcome
- Negotiating as if the settlement will hold across the next full cycle

### Counterexamples

- Where one party's dominance is already established and demonstrated, a negotiated exchange under-values that party's position and delays settlement.

### Source citations

## Zero-Bound Policy Triage

> Determines when conventional rate cuts have been exhausted and the policy problem shifts from easing to other levers.

- book: 
- lenses: economics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- Policy rates are near zero or constrained by currency and credit risk
- Rate cuts are no longer producing wealth effects, activity, or debt-service relief
- A debate exists over whether the situation is a recession or a depression

### Inputs

- Current policy rate and the credit-risk and currency-risk floor on that rate
- Observed response of lending, spending, and asset prices to recent cuts
- Scale of debt-service obligations relative to income

### Steps

- Establish whether the imbalance is between money available and money needed to service debt.
- Estimate the effective floor on interest rates, including any credit or currency risk premium.
- Test whether further cuts still produce a wealth effect, activity stimulus, and debt-service relief.
- If those channels are broken, label the situation a depression-type deleveraging rather than a normal recession.
- Shift attention to alternative means of stimulating, as in the well-managed 2007-08 US case.

### Outputs

- A classification of the downturn as recession-type or depression-type
- A statement of the binding constraint on monetary policy
- A shortlist of alternative levers to consider

### Failure modes

- Assuming the zero bound is the only floor and ignoring currency-risk-driven higher floors
- Continuing to advocate rate cuts after the transmission channels have stopped working
- Inferring a durable recovery from a policy action that has not yet changed credit availability

### Counterexamples

- In normal recessions where monetary policy is still effective, the same triage would wrongly escalate to unconventional measures and misread a self-correcting slowdown as a depression.

### Source citations

> In normal recessions (when monetary policy is still effective), the imbalance between the amount of money and the need for it to service debt can be rectified by cutting interest rates enough to 1) produce a positive wealth effect, 2) stimulate economic activity, and 3) ease debt-service burdens.
  -  · p.23
> To us the economy was now in the classic early days of a deleveraging/depres-sion, when monetary policy could not work normally.
  -  · p.227

## 三角外交杠杆测算

> 在三方博弈中评估结盟与解盟的收益:用一方牵制另一方,并计算经济杠杆(市场、贸易、人权挂钩)在其中的可兑换性。

- book: 
- lenses: economics, politics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- 分析冷战及后冷战时期的大国三角关系
- 评估经济制裁或优惠待遇作为外交筹码的效果
- 判断联盟是理念性的还是事实需要性的

### Inputs

- 三方各自的军力部署与经济相互依赖度
- 可动用的经济工具(最惠国、市场准入、赔款退款)
- 国内政治对让步的容忍度与面子成本
- 对方的替代选项与承受力

### Steps

- 先判定现有同盟是理念产物还是事实需要
- 列出每一方最忌惮的牵制点(边境驻军、市场、联盟)
- 把经济工具折算为双方损失,识别双输区间
- 寻找保住面子的替代方案(脱钩、针对性制裁、局部让步)
- 预判杠杆使用后第三方的反应(如日本的正常化加速)
- 评估三角关系中的平衡位置是否被重新确认

### Outputs

- 杠杆使用的得失对照表
- 保住面子的替代方案清单
- 三角格局的稳定性判断

### Failure modes

- 把人权或价值议题当作可无限使用的杠杆,忽略对方的承受极限
- 低估对手看穿虚张声势后的反制空间

### Counterexamples

- 当经济相互依赖不对称且对方无替代市场时,同一杠杆的效力会大幅下降,而非相反

### Source citations

## 党规—国法双重约束

> 在中国政府与政治中，行为合法性同时受国家法律和党内法规约束；分析法律问题必须把党规、党委文件与党政关系纳入。

- book: 
- lenses: law, politics
- confidence: medium · status: verified

### Applicable when

- 分析监管权限与政策合法性
- 判断执法、司法与地方治理风险
- 做合规与政治风险双重评估

### Inputs

- 法律与行政法规
- 党内法规与党委文件
- 机构权限与授权链条
- 司法审查与问责机制

### Steps

- 列明国家法上的权限依据。
- 列明党规与党委文件上的依据。
- 判断两者关系、冲突解决与效力层级。
- 确认执行主体是政府、党委还是两者共同。
- 评估法律问责与政治问责的双重风险，并注明不是法律意见。

### Outputs

- 双重合法性矩阵
- 权限边界与风险提示

### Failure modes

- 只看法条而忽略党规与党委文件。
- 把政策文件直接当作法律依据。
- 法律修订滞后或地方执行差异。

### Counterexamples

- 纯技术性、低政治敏感度的行政事项，国法可能是主要约束。
- 党内法规未公开时，外部分析只能给出条件性判断。

### Counterexamples

- 与利润挂钩的所得税是典型的税,不满足租的判别条件
